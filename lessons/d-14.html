Two Pointers Deep — Fast & Slow, Three Sum

Opposite ends, different speeds, triplets without duplicates

1. Opposite ends: the sorted-array workhorse

▶ YouTube reference — the video for this lesson

With sorted input, two pointers at opposite ends turn pair problems linear. If the current pair sums too small, the left pointer must move right — nothing smaller can work with this right element; if too large, the right pointer moves left. Every step eliminates one candidate permanently, which is why the scan is O(n) after the O(n log n) sort. Container With Most Water uses the same skeleton with a different rule: always move the shorter wall, because the taller one cannot increase the area with any partner closer in.

2. Fast and slow: cycles and middles

▶ YouTube reference — the video for this lesson

Fast and slow pointers move in the same direction at different speeds — usually two steps versus one. On a linked list, if a cycle exists the fast pointer laps the slow one and they meet; if not, fast reaches the end. When fast finishes, slow sits at the middle, which is how you split a list in half without knowing its length. The same idea finds the start of a cycle: after the meeting point, restart one pointer at the head and move both one step at a time — they meet exactly at the cycle entrance, a fact worth proving to yourself once with distances.

3. 3Sum: sort, fix one, two-pointer the rest

▶ YouTube reference — the video for this lesson

3Sum asks for all unique triplets summing to zero. Sort the array, fix each element in turn as the first of the triplet, then run the opposite-ends two-pointer scan on the remainder looking for pairs that sum to the negation of the fixed element. Sorting does double duty: it enables the two-pointer scan and groups duplicates together so they can be skipped. Total cost is O(n²) — n fixed choices times a linear scan — against O(n³) brute force.

4. Deduplication: where 3Sum solutions actually fail

▶ YouTube reference — the video for this lesson

The algorithm is easy; the duplicates are the exam. Skip a fixed element if it equals the previous one. After finding a valid triplet, advance both pointers and skip over any values equal to the ones just used. Miss any of the three skip rules and the answer contains duplicate triplets. The discipline generalises: whenever sorting is used to enable a pointer pattern, decide the duplicate policy explicitly before coding, and test on inputs like all zeros, where duplicates dominate.

def three_sum(nums):
    nums.sort()
    result = []
    n = len(nums)
    for i in range(n - 2):
        if i > 0 and nums[i] == nums[i - 1]:
            continue
        left, right = i + 1, n - 1
        while left < right:
            total = nums[i] + nums[left] + nums[right]
            if total == 0:
                result.append([nums[i], nums[left], nums[right]])
                left += 1
                right -= 1
                while left < right and nums[left] == nums[left - 1]:
                    left += 1
                while left < right and nums[right] == nums[right + 1]:
                    right -= 1
            elif total < 0:
                left += 1
            else:
                right -= 1
    return result

# Sort O(n log n), then n linear scans: O(n^2) time, O(1) extra space.

In Container With Most Water, why always move the shorter wall?

The area is limited by the shorter wall and the width only shrinks as pointers move in, so keeping the shorter wall can never beat the current area. Moving it is the only chance to improve.

How do fast and slow pointers find the middle of a list in one pass?

Fast moves two steps per slow step, so when fast reaches the end, slow has covered half the distance and sits at the middle.

After fast and slow meet inside a cycle, how do you find the cycle entrance?

Restart one pointer at the head and move both one step at a time; they meet at the entrance, because the distance from head to entrance equals the distance from the meeting point to entrance.

What are the three duplicate-skip rules in 3Sum?

Skip a fixed element equal to the previous one, and after recording a triplet skip repeated values at both the left and right pointers.

Bonus round — flashcards (+5 XP each)

Define each term out loud before revealing. These count toward your score below.

What is “Opposite ends”?

Pointers starting at both ends of a sorted array, moving inward by a comparison rule — the classic pair pattern.

What is “Fast and slow pointers”?

Two pointers at different speeds, usually 2:1, used for cycle detection, middles and cycle entrances.

What is “Floyd's cycle detection”?

Fast laps slow inside a cycle, proving one exists in O(n) time and O(1) space.

What is “3Sum pattern”?

Sort, fix one element, then two-pointer the remainder for pairs summing to its negation — O(n²).

What is “Deduplication”?

Skipping equal values after sorting so each unique answer is produced exactly once.

What is “Cycle entrance”?

Found by restarting one pointer at the head after a meeting and advancing both one step at a time.

7 · Interview ▾

Why does sorting make 3Sum O(n²) instead of O(n³)?

Fixing one element leaves a pair-sum problem on sorted input, which two pointers solve in O(n). Doing that for each of n elements gives O(n²), and sorting also makes deduplication trivial.

Prove that fast and slow pointers must meet inside a cycle.

Once both are in the cycle, fast gains one step on slow per iteration, so the gap shrinks by one each time and must reach zero — they cannot jump over each other.
